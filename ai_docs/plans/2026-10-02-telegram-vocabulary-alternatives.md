# Vocabulary teaching points in Telegram

Status: historical local revision from 2026-10-20; not deployed or pushed. Superseded by the [current two-suggestion Vocabulary rule](2026-10-20-vocabulary-rebuild-draft.md). All three-category rules below are historical. The [larger probe](../researches/2026-10-20-live-vocabulary-larger-sample.md) found low alternative recall and weak verifier filtering. A subsequent [real-conversation replay](../researches/2026-10-20-real-vocabulary-level-up-replay.md) found that the revised prompt still creates weak alternatives which the final verifier accepts. This revision is not ready to deploy. The earlier final-review-only proposal and its failed synthetic probe are preserved in [the probe](../researches/2026-10-20-vocabulary-mixed-assessment-prompt-probe.md).

## Decision

The same live notes request checks each learner turn for three independent kinds of teaching point: definite Grammar/Vocabulary errors; established idiomatic expressions that upgrade an already correct phrase; and context-appropriate, more precise vocabulary that upgrades an already correct plain choice. An optional alternative must represent a concrete increase in expressive range. A same-level paraphrase, rarer word for its own sake, register shift, or change in meaning does not qualify. The alternative is never a mistake or direct evidence for a lower score. Empty output is valid.

The notes model makes three independent passes: errors, idiomatic upgrades, then lexical upgrades in correct phrases. The presence of an error in another clause does not block an alternative. Both kinds pass exact transcript grounding, ASR/artifact checks, local edit overlap checks, and type-specific flags. Alternatives use `kind=alternative`, `wrong` as the original phrase, `better` as the optional phrase, and the Russian explanation generated at the turn. The one live Telegram card prioritizes Grammar, then incorrect Vocabulary, then optional alternatives. The alternative card does not strike through the original.

Every newly generated note also declares exactly one `vocabularyCategory`: `vocabulary_error` for `kind=word` or `natural`, `native_expression` for an established expression, or `word_upgrade` for a higher-level word. Grammar notes declare `null`. The parser rejects missing or incompatible labels. The label is stored with the correction and passed to the final verifier, which checks the claim independently. Legacy saved candidates without a label remain eligible for review. The category is internal audit data; it does not change the Telegram card or assessment copy.

Examples: `the advantages and disadvantages` → `the pros and cons` teaches an established pair; `used what we had` → `made do with what we had` requires evidence of limited resources; `increased a lot` → `increased substantially` is a context-specific lexical upgrade. `switch the topic` → `change the subject` and `when I had free time` → `in my spare time` are same-level paraphrases, so they do not qualify. A mechanical `very` → `significantly` substitution can be unnatural and must be judged in the full sentence. The live prompt and final alternative verifier now use these boundaries.

All candidates are saved on onboarding and practice-call turns. The final verifier receives separate batches for errors and alternatives because their acceptance criteria differ. A failed batch cannot erase successful batches. The final assessment selects at most five verified Grammar errors, five verified Vocabulary errors, and three verified Vocabulary alternatives. Both onboarding and ordinary practice calls use this path. The final review model writes assessment text and scores from the conversation; it no longer invents new `vocabularySuggestions`. The Vocabulary slide shows verified alternatives beside verified corrections and reuses their live explanations. Completed cached reviews are not regenerated.

## Validation and limits

The local Python suite passed 385 tests. Focused Kotlin Telegram tests and detekt passed. Isolated provider probes on four synthetic turns with the first prompt returned zero alternatives. A two-pass prompt with three concrete positive examples returned alternatives on three relevant turns and none on the natural-control turn. It found an error and an independent alternative in the same sentence. An error-focused mixed verifier rejected both good alternatives; a separate alternative verifier accepted both in an isolated probe. In a follow-up, the verifier initially accepted an unsupported `make do with` option. An explicit resource-constraint rule made it reject that option while retaining the valid `on the side` option and rejecting a stage-changing `launch` rewrite. These requests used the configured PROD provider but did not create or change chats, call records, or assessments.

The probes show the route can work; they do not measure usefulness or false positives on real speech. One generated suggestion in the clean-turn probe (`use what I have` → `make do with what I have`) overstated the evidence for a resource constraint. The live prompt and final verifier now require explicit evidence of limited resources; the verifier rejected that example in a repeat probe. Real-audio review remains necessary before rollout. The existing 1200-token notes budget and 10-second deadline remain. A timeout still loses that turn's teaching points.

The 2026-10-20 level-up prompt probe used the configured provider without changing production data. Five direct positive examples, one mixed error-plus-alternative turn, and four negative controls produced the intended kinds of teaching points. A separate ten-turn set with close paraphrases yielded the intended six positives and four empty negatives. After adding more diverse examples, two newly represented situations (`ask for a raise`, `make up`) produced alternatives. Four other plausible idiomatic upgrades, including `kept the project secret` → `kept the project under wraps` and `could not choose between two job offers` → `was torn between two job offers`, still produced no notes. The separate final verifier accepted two valid example-based candidates and rejected three same-level or unsupported candidates. These small synthetic probes show that the prompt can enforce the requested distinctions in tested cases, but it still misses unseen expressions. They do not establish real-conversation recall or readiness to deploy.

The later category-field probe used the same provider routes in isolation. Seven controlled turns produced a Vocabulary error labelled `vocabulary_error`, an established pair labelled `native_expression`, a higher-level fear word labelled `word_upgrade`, and a Grammar error labelled `null`; two negative controls produced no notes. The final verifier accepted `feel scared` → `feel frightened` and `put makeup on my face` → `put on makeup`, while rejecting `begun` → `started`, `a little bit rusty` → `a little rusty`, and `hanging out` → `catching up` in the tested contexts. On 30 selected real turns, every returned note had a category consistent with its `kind`, but the model proposed zero optional alternatives. Classification is therefore auditable; it does not by itself solve alternative recall or guarantee semantic quality.
